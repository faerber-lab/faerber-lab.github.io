  1. From a Web of Documents to a Web of Claims: A Claim Layer for Human and AI Science
    Jingbo He, and Michael Färber
    In ISWC, 2026
    For insiders: A claim-level scholarly graph would give scientific assertions stable identities and support reasoning about which claims can hold together.
    For everyone: The paper proposes a way to identify and compare individual research claims together with their evidence and conditions.
    Abstract DOI PDF

    We propose the Web of Claims as the next stage of the Web for science: after documents and data, claims. Scoped to the scholarly record, every scientific assertion becomes a first-class object with a persistent identifier, a signature, stated conditions, and an equivalence class over its surface forms. Two halves of this system exist separately today: the Semantic Web community solved the representation of such assertions, but the infrastructure was never populated at scale; generative AI has made population cheap, but its pipelines discard the claims they extract after every run. Conditioned claims form a graph that neither earlier Web contains, and over it a third kind of inference becomes definable, acceptability: which claims can be jointly held, under which conditions, on what evidence. A running example develops the claim and this inference step by step. Both rest on two open problems, claim identity and contextual truth; we show against four criteria that no existing line of work solves all four, and outline a twenty-five-year research agenda.

  2. From Universal Knowledge Graphs to Contextual Semantic Contracts
    Himanshu Beniwal, and Michael Färber
    In ISWC, 2026
    For insiders: The paper proposes making context an explicit part of how Semantic Web systems represent and interpret knowledge.
    For everyone: A fact can mean different things in different settings. The paper argues that AI systems should represent those settings explicitly.
    Abstract DOI PDF

    The Semantic Web has transformed the Web into a machine-readable ecosystem through ontologies, linked data, and knowledge graphs, enabling structured representation and reasoning over factual knowledge. However, the emergence of foundation models and generative AI has fundamentally changed how knowledge is consumed, generated, and interpreted. Modern AI systems increasingly fail not because they lack factual knowledge, but because they struggle to interpret that knowledge under diverse temporal, cultural, legal, and social contexts. We argue that this limitation stems from a deeper representational gap: the Semantic Web primarily models facts, while future AI requires explicit representations of contextual meaning. We envision the next generation of semantic technologies as evolving from a Web of Facts to a Web of Context, where context becomes a first-class semantic object that governs interpretation rather than merely annotating it. We outline a research agenda for context-centric semantic representations and argue that representing meaning, rather than facts alone, should become the defining challenge for the next twenty-five years of Semantic Web research.

  3. Does Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) Dominate Arabic Dialects in LLMs? A Representation-Level Analysis
    Abdu Sallouh, Nicholas Popovič, and Michael Färber
    In EMNLP, 2026
    For insiders: Across 26 Arabic varieties, the study finds no evidence that Modern Standard Arabic dominates the internal representations of dialects.
    For everyone: Language models often answer in standard Arabic, but their internal representations do not show that standard Arabic dominates the dialects they process.
    Abstract DOI PDF

    Large language models (LLMs) often default to Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) when generating Arabic, even when prompted with dialectal Arabic. A natural explanation is that their internal representations are dominated by MSA. We test this hypothesis by adapting the language-dominance framework of Shani and Basirat (2025) to 26 Arabic varieties. Across layers and model families, we find no evidence that MSA acts as a dominant internal representation for Arabic dialects. Instead, dialect representations form a dense and highly overlapping space: normalized mutual information drops sharply compared to patterns reported for more distinct languages. Moreover, the strongest separability effects are not confined to intermediate layers, but can shift toward later layers depending on the architecture. These findings challenge a common interpretation of MSA-biased generation: output preference does not necessarily reveal internal representational dominance. Analyses of multilingual and dialectal LLMs should therefore distinguish generation bias from the geometry of internal representations.

  4. Energy-Aware Benchmarking of Generative Information Extraction
    Andrei Politov, Iryna Vergunova, Rene Jäkel, and 1 more author
    In EMNLP, 2026
    For insiders: The benchmark measures how decoding constraints, output formats, and serving settings affect energy use and extraction accuracy.
    For everyone: The authors measure how much energy language models use to extract structured facts and how their settings affect the result.
    Abstract DOI PDF

    Using Large Language Models (LLMs) for Information Extraction (IE) inherently involves transforming diverse textual inputs into strictly formatted outputs. Guaranteeing this structural validity introduces a hidden energy tax driven by constrained decoding mechanisms, serialisation formats, and underlying serving configurations. Consequently, standard token-level metrics fail to capture the true energy cost of generative IE, which also cannot be predicted by model size alone. To address this, we introduce the first dedicated benchmark evaluating the energy-accuracy trade-offs of generative IE. Our framework establishes task-aligned energy metrics, including Energy per True Positive (J/TP) and the F1-Energy Ratio (F_1/kJ). Through comprehensive ablation across architectures, decoding regimes, and serving-layer limits, we demonstrate that efficiency is governed by complex parameter interactions. We translate these findings into actionable configuration guidelines, equipping practitioners to maximise predictive accuracy, optimise energy yield, or achieve a sustainable Pareto balance. The source code and evaluation results are available on GitHub\textsuperscript1.

  5. SciEmbed: Citation-Context Supervision for Scientific Document Embeddings
    Jonas Wilinski, and Michael Färber
    In EMNLP, 2026
    For insiders: SciEmbed trains a scientific document encoder on citation-context sentences and evaluates the resulting model on SciRepEval.
    For everyone: The model learns to represent papers from the sentences in which other researchers cite them.
    Abstract DOI PDF

    Open scientific document embedders are typically trained on binary citation edges between paper title-abstract pairs. This signal discards the citation context sentence: a domain-expert description of the cited paper, written by the citing author. We use these sentences as the primary contrastive supervision signal to train SciEmbed, an open 149M ModernBERT-base encoder. On the official SciRepEval benchmark it reaches 66.85 over three seeds, surpassing prior open scientific embedders such as SPECTER2 and SciNCL. To isolate what the supervision source contributes, we compare against IBM Granite R2, a same-size ModernBERT peer trained on general retrieval data. Using only open citation-context data, SciEmbed matches Granite R2 to within 0.6 on the SciRepEval macro overall while specialising in the opposite direction: it leads regression tasks tied to paper impact by 2.3 points, where Granite R2 instead leads classification, proximity, and search. A 28-configuration sweep over recipes and alternative supervision signals does not improve on this result, consistent with the specialisation tracking the supervision source rather than the training recipe. Training at longer contexts yields monotonic gains on a body-fact retrieval probe within our recipe. We release the model, training pipeline, and evaluation harness as a fully open, citation-context-supervised baseline with a distinct, reproducible specialisation pattern.

  6. Gold vs. Translation: Are We Measuring Language, Knowledge, or Locality in Translated Benchmarks?
    Klaudia-Doris Thellmann, Bernhard Stadler, Dan Saattrup Smart, and 2 more authors
    In EMNLP, 2026
    For insiders: Translated MMLU scores better predict performance on native benchmarks when the tested content overlaps.
    For everyone: The paper tests when a translated exam can stand in for a test written in the target language and where it misses local knowledge.
    Abstract DOI PDF

    Translated versions of English-origin benchmarks such as MMLU are widely used to evaluate large language models in non-English languages, yet it remains unclear whether they measure the same constructs as natively created benchmarks. We frame this as a proxy validity question and compare DeepL-translated MMLU proxies against six native benchmarks in seven pairings across five Nordic and Baltic languages, evaluating 29 models on each pairing. Beyond ranking agreement, we test incremental proxy validity: does the translated score add predictive value for native performance after controlling for model size and language proficiency? Proxy validity varies substantially across benchmark pairings. Applying the same robustness criteria to all seven pairings, we find four robust effects, two marginal effects, and one null result. The strongest evidence occurs for curriculum-overlap benchmarks such as Skolprov (∆R^2 = .128, 95% CI [.038, .253]), whereas translated MMLU provides no significant incremental value for Sweden-specific factual knowledge in SwedishFacts (p = .242). Because SwedishFacts is also the only open-ended benchmark, we cannot fully separate the effects of local content and response format. Overall, the results indicate a clear pattern: translated benchmarks provide a better proxy when their source content overlaps with the native evaluation target, while locally anchored knowledge is less reliably captured.

  7. Example-Guided Prompting for Document-Level Text Simplification
    Ariel Perstin, Ilan Shtilman, Michael Färber, and 1 more author
    In INLG, 2026
    Author order and abstract follow the supplied conference manuscript. URL points to the arXiv preprint
    For insiders: Adding retrieved simplification examples to prompts generally improves document-level simplification on OneStopEnglish.
    For everyone: The system shows a language model relevant examples before asking it to simplify a full document.
    Abstract PDF

    Document-level text simplification requires large language models (LLMs) to rewrite complex documents while preserving meaning, readability, and discourse coherence. Although prompt-based LLMs have shown promising performance, they often produce inconsistent simplifications because textual instructions alone provide limited guidance for complex document-level transformations. We investigate whether retrieved document-simplification examples can improve document-level generation by augmenting prompts with examples selected from a parallel simplification corpus. This example-guided prompting approach enables LLMs to exploit relevant simplification patterns without task-specific fine-tuning. Experiments on the OneStopEnglish corpus using multiple state-of-the-art LLMs show that incorporating retrieved examples generally improves simplification quality over prompt-only generation and achieves competitive or superior performance compared with representative supervised (T5) and planning-based (PlanSimp) document simplification systems. Furthermore, we find that the benefits of example-guided prompting vary across LLMs, suggesting that effective use of retrieved examples depends on a model’s ability to integrate contextual information during generation.

  8. Scientific Claim-Source Retrieval Revisited: A Comparative Study of Style Transfer and Re-ranking
    Tobias Schreieder, Harsh Khandelwal, Yu-Ling Zhong, and 1 more author
    In CLEF, 2026
    For insiders: A comparison on CheckThat! 2026 evaluates query translation, metadata, style transfer, and re-ranking, with verification-based re-ranking yielding the highest MRR@5.
    For everyone: The authors compare methods for finding the original paper behind a scientific claim shared online.
    Abstract DOI PDF

    Scientific claims shared on social media are often difficult to verify and may contribute to the spread of misinformation. To address this challenge, automated fact verification systems require scientific claim–source retrieval, the task of identifying the source publication underlying a given claim. However, claims often differ substantially from their source publications in language, style, and specificity, making retrieval challenging. We present a comparative study of scientific claim–source retrieval on the CheckThat! 2026 benchmark across sparse and dense retrieval models. Our results show that translating claims into English outperforms both original and bilingual claim representations, while incorporating publication metadata provides additional retrieval gains by capturing indirect source references. In addition, we analyze four style transfer approaches and find that they improve retrieval performance for most models, although the optimal style depends on the underlying retrieval objective. Finally, we investigate similarity- and signal-based re-ranking approaches, introducing three novel re-ranking models based on attribution, entity overlap, and verification-based reasoning. Verification-based re-ranking yields additional gains beyond semantic similarity and achieves the best overall performance with an MRR@5 of 0.758.

  9. Claim2Source at CheckThat! 2026: Improving Multilingual Scientific Claim–Source Retrieval with Verification-based Re-Ranking
    Tobias Schreieder, Harsh Khandelwal, Yu-Ling Zhong, and 1 more author
    In CLEF, 2026
    For insiders: A multilingual retrieval pipeline uses verification to re-rank papers and placed first on CheckThat! 2026 Task 1.
    For everyone: The system finds the research paper behind a claim and checks which candidate best supports it.
    Abstract PDF

    Multilingual scientific claim–source retrieval aims to identify the scientific publication supporting a claim shared on social media. This task is challenging because claims often differ from source publications in terms of language, wording, and level of detail, which weakens the connection between claims and their underlying evidence. In this paper, we present our approach for the CheckThat! 2026 Lab Task 1: Source Retrieval for Scientific Web Claims. We propose a multi-stage retrieval framework for multilingual scientific claim–source retrieval that combines structured claim and source representations with progressive candidate refinement. To address multilingual retrieval challenges, the framework employs bilingual claim representations, metadata-enhanced source representations, and language-specific adaptation of dense retrieval models. Our approach achieves an average MRR@5 score of 0.7628 across English, German, and French claims, ranking first on the CheckThat! 2026 leaderboard.

  11. RCT: A Robot-Collected Touch-Vision-Language Dataset for Tactile Generalization
    Jingbo He, Michael Färber, and Roberto Calandra
    CoRR, 2026
    For insiders: RCT combines robot-collected touch data with visual and linguistic information for tactile generalization.
    For everyone: The dataset pairs what a robot feels with what it sees and how the object can be described.
    Abstract

    For robots manipulating open-world objects, tactile representations must generalize to unseen materials. We introduce RCT (Robotic Contact Tactile), a robot-collected touch-vision-language dataset with 29,279 tactile frames from full robot presses on 122 industrial reference materials in 7 categories, recorded with three DIGIT sensors at multiple contact positions. RCT preserves each press as a contact sequence, enabling held-out evaluation across materials, categories, sensors, contact positions, and contact sequences. Frames from one press are strongly correlated: frame-random splits can place near-duplicate observations of the same physical interaction in both training and test. With the encoder held fixed, removing contact-sequence overlap reduces tactile-to-text Recall@1 by 17.7 percentage points. When materials are additionally held out at training time, performance drops sharply, leaving held-out-material Recall@1 at 25.1 +/- 6.1% averaged over three held-out draws. The public TVL/HCT split shows the same structure: every test contact sequence appears in training, and raw-pixel nearest neighbors recover the correct sequence in 98.3% of cases. Uniformly sampling a press improves contrastive training, and RCT-trained embeddings improve category probes on unseen materials. RCT makes contact-sequence-aware, held-out-material evaluation reproducible and exposes novel-material generalization as a central challenge for robotic tactile perception. The RCT dataset is open-sourced at https://faerber-lab.github.io/RCT/

  12. Topic-to-Timestamp Alignment by Constrained Evidence Selection
    Zeynep Yılbırt, Marina Litvak, and Michael Färber
    CoRR, 2026
    For insiders: The method locates meeting topics by choosing among retrieved timestamped transcript segments.
    For everyone: Given a meeting topic, the system finds where in the recording people discussed it.
    Abstract

    Meeting archives are difficult to search when users remember what was discussed but not when. We study topic-to-timestamp alignment: given a natural-language topic and a timestamped meeting transcript, the goal is to return the time at which the topic is discussed. A standard RAG setup can retrieve relevant transcript excerpts, but still asks the language model to generate a timestamp, which can produce unsupported or invalid timecodes. We therefore recast timestamp prediction as constrained temporal candidate selection: the system retrieves timestamped transcript chunks, and the model selects the candidate that best grounds the topic instead of generating a timecode. On 420 topic-timestamp queries from 200 municipal meeting transcripts, this increases Recall@5 from 31.9% to 50.0%, reduces MAE from 837.0 seconds to 761.0 seconds with Mistral-7B-Instruct, and increases the number of parseable outputs from 373 to 419 of 420 queries. The results suggest that temporal grounding in long transcripts depends strongly on retrieval quality and output design, not only on the choice of the language model.

  15. EchoRL: Reinforcement Learning via Rollout Echoing
    Jinhe Bi,  Aniri, Minglai Yang, and 9 more authors
    In ICML, 2026
    For insiders: EchoRL improves RLVR post-training by reusing entropy-selected clips from verified-success rollouts whose standard advantage signal has degenerated.
    For everyone: This paper helps language models keep learning during reinforcement learning even when many generated answers are already correct and the usual training signal disappears.
    Abstract PDF

    Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards is an effective route for post-training to strengthen the reasoning capability of large language models. However, as training proceeds, the learning signal can collapse thus makes the training gain become marginal and ineffective. Specifically, a growing fraction of prompts’ rollouts become advantage-degenerated: all the self-generated rollouts show verified-success, making the standard deviation over their rewards be zero; accordingly each rollout’s advantage becomes degenerated (zero) as well. Given such rollouts’ advantages, the policy-gradient for model optimization eventually vanishes, capping the training performance. We argue that some of these rollouts still contain valuable learning signals but unfortunately omitted with the existing RLVR methods. In this paper, inspired through analyzing the entropy pattern behind golden trajectories produced by external expert models, we propose EchoRL for better exploiting the advantage-degenerated rollouts to further improve the training performance. EchoRL is a lightweight module that first identifies an EchoClip from verified-success rollouts based on their step-level entropy values, and then feeds this clip back as an auxiliary supervision signal in the RL objective. Extensive experiments across 10 benchmarks, 5 LLM backbones, and 4 popular RLVR post-training methods demonstrate that EchoRL consistently improves RLVR post-training with minimal overhead.

  16. X-VORTEX: Spatio-Temporal Contrastive Learning for Wake Vortex Trajectory Forecasting
    Zhan Qu, and Michael Färber
    In KDD, Jeju Island, Republic of Korea, 2026
    For insiders: We introduce X-VORTEX, a self-supervised spatio-temporal contrastive learning framework for wake vortex localization and trajectory forecasting from LiDAR point cloud sequences.
    For everyone: Aircraft create invisible wake vortices that can be dangerous for following planes. X-VORTEX learns from large LiDAR datasets to track and predict these vortices with very little labeled data.
    Abstract DOI PDF

    Wake vortices are strong, coherent air turbulences created by aircraft, and they pose a major safety and capacity challenge for air traffic management. Tracking how vortices move, weaken, and dissipate over time from LiDAR measurements is still difficult because scans are sparse, vortex signatures fade as the flow breaks down under atmospheric turbulence and instabilities, and point-wise annotation is prohibitively expensive. Existing approaches largely treat each scan as an independent, fully supervised segmentation problem, which overlooks temporal structure and does not scale to the vast unlabeled archives collected in practice. We present X-VORTEX, a spatio-temporal contrastive learning framework grounded in Augmentation Overlap Theory that learns physics-aware representations from unlabeled LiDAR point cloud sequences. X-VORTEX addresses two core challenges: sensor sparsity and time-varying vortex dynamics. It constructs paired inputs from the same underlying flight event by combining a weakly perturbed sequence with a strongly augmented counterpart produced via temporal subsampling and spatial masking, encouraging the model to align representations across missing frames and partial observations. Architecturally, a time-distributed geometric encoder extracts per-scan features and a sequential aggregator models the evolving vortex state across variable-length sequences. We evaluate on a real-world dataset of over one million LiDAR scans. X-VORTEX achieves superior vortex center localization while using only 1% of the labeled data required by supervised baselines, and the learned representations support accurate trajectory forecasting.
